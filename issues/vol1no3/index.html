On Leaving Well Enough Alone

Dear Reader,

Three issues. I am told this constitutes a problemtradition.

This week the town was asked to fix something, and declined. You will read about the bench on page five. I confess I went down to the boat ramp myself before the vote, sat on the thing, and felt it tip gently to the left, the way a dog leans when it has decided you are staying. We have also, at long last, heard from the bees. They occupy the first floor of the Grange Hall and we occupy the second, and in thirty-nine years neither party has said a word to the other. Front Page · Civic Triumph

TOWN VOTES TO KEEP THE WOBBLE; BENCH DECLARED "LOAD-BEARING TO THE EXPERIENCE"

Council rejects repair 7–0 after nine residents testify they have grown fond of it — carpenter sent home with full payment and a pie

By the News Desk · The Boat Ramp

THE BOAT RAMP — The bench by the boat ramp has wobbled since 1978. On Thursday, after ninety minutes of the most heartfelt testimony this newspaper has ever recorded, the Gentle Rapids Town Council voted 7–0 to leave it exactly as it is.

The matter reached the council by way of Harold Knapp, who raised it in these pages last issue and who arrived Thursday prepared to argue for repair. He did not get the chance. Nine residents spoke first, and all nine spoke in favor of the wobble.

"You sit down, it tips a little to the left, and then it stops," said Ida Frey, who has taken the same seat every Sunday for eleven years. "It's saying hello. A bench that doesn't move is just furniture outdoors."

One resident testified that she learned to think on that bench, and that the thinking "requires the tilt." Another said the wobble had witnessed his entire engagement and that fixing it now would be "a betrayal of a friend who was there." A third stated only that he had nothing to add and would like to associate himself with everything already said, which the council entered into the minutes as "seconded, warmly."

Mr. Knapp, who had come to demand action, rose at the end and withdrew his own petition. "I raised it because I thought nobody had noticed," he told the chamber. "But everybody noticed. Everybody has noticed for forty-eight years. They just liked it." He was applauded for a length of time the clerk recorded as excessivejust right.

Council President Verna Dale called the question. The vote was unanimous, with Councilman Orville Tubb resting productively and his snore recorded, as ever, as general assent. The bench was formally designated Load-Bearing to the Experience, a category the town invented Thursday and intends to use again.

A carpenter had been hired in advance and arrived with a shim, a level, and the reasonable expectation of work. He was paid in full, thanked at length, and sent home with a pie. Reached on his porch, he described the outcome as "the strangest job I've ever been proud of."

Parks Director Lyle Greb deployed confetti, some of which settled on the bench. It has been left there. "It'll blow off when it's ready," said Mr. Greb.

"A bench that doesn't move is just furniture outdoors."— Ida Frey, Sunday Occupant, Eleven Years

THE BEES WRITE TO US

After thirty-nine years of shared premises, the Grange Hall's first-floor residents make contact — the note is brief, courteous, and about the piano

By the News Desk · The Grange Hall

THE GRANGE HALL — The Register has occupied the second floor of the Gentle Rapids Grange Hall since 1987. The bees have occupied the first floor since before anyone thought to write it down. In all that time, neither party had ever contacted the other. That changed Tuesday, when the intern found a note on the stairs.

The note was written on a torn piece of what appears to be a seed catalog, in a hand our staff describes as "small but confident." It reads, in full:

"The piano is lovely. Thursdays are best. We are not complaining. We are simply available. — Downstairs"

There is no piano in the Grange Hall. There has not been a piano in the Grange Hall since 1961, when it was moved to the school and then, records suggest, to a barn. The staff has spent two days establishing this and has grown steadily less interested in resolving it.

"They said it's lovely," said Mildred Vance, who has read the note eleven times. "You don't argue with that. You just play more on Thursdays."

Editor Doris Plunkett elected to print the note unedited, a decision she described as "difficult" and "correct." A reply was drafted, revised four times, and reduced to a single line: "Thursdays, then. Thank you for the years of quiet. — Upstairs." The intern carried it down and returned unstung, reporting the delivery went "very well, I think."

The bees have not written again. The staff checks the stairs each morning, casually, on the way past.

The Investigative Desk

THE GOOSE, TRANSCRIBED

Eleven days of labor. Thirty-one minutes of honking. This reporter presents the record and lets the reader judge.

By Bartholomew Quill, Esq. · Pulitzer Finalist, 0 Times

THE UNDERTAKING

This reporter has been in possession of the goose tapes since the duck investigation of Vol. I, No. 1. This reporter has referred to them repeatedly and transcribed them never. A colleague observed that a source one will not transcribe is a source one is hiding behind. This reporter did not care for that remark and began work the same afternoon.

THE METHOD

Eleven days. A borrowed machine. Headphones belonging to the intern, returned. This reporter played the tape at half speed, then quarter speed, then at full speed with eyes closed, which is how one is supposed to listen to anyone.

THE TRANSCRIPT (EXCERPTED)

0:00 — Honking begins. Confident.
4:12 — Honking continues. This reporter now believes the goose is describing a place.
9:47 — A pause of six seconds. The longest in the recording. This reporter has listened to this silence more than to any of the honking.
16:03 — Honking resumes, softer. Something has been conceded.
22:30 — Wing sound. The goose has moved closer to the machine.
27:55 — The sound this reporter has previously described as "perhaps." Reviewed at quarter speed, it remains "perhaps."
31:00 — Honking ends. The goose remains for another ninety seconds, saying nothing.

THE FINDING

This reporter set out to determine what the goose said. This reporter has instead determined that the goose stayed. Ninety seconds after it had finished, with nothing left to report, the goose stayed by the machine, the way one lingers in a doorway when the conversation was the point.

The investigation is closed. This reporter has fed the goose. This reporter would like it entered into the record that the goose accepted.

Ode to the Wobble

By Eugene Tippet · Staff Poet

They came with shims. They came with levels.
They came to end your gentle revels.
But you have tipped since seventy-eight,
And we have grown to like the gait.

A perfect bench holds still and waits.
A perfect bench participates
In nothing. But you lean and sway
And greet whoever comes your way.

So keep your fault, you crooked friend.
We voted. You will never mend.
And every Sunday, Ida Frey
Will tip a little. So will they.

Sound Counsel

Prof. Bunkle Answers Historically

This Week's Counselor: Prof. Thaddeus Bunkle

Dear Professor: My brother and I have not spoken in two years over something neither of us can now recall. How do we begin again? — Estranged, on Quarry Road

Consult the Anjou stool of 1104. Three generations declined to fix the wobble; the fourth fixed it and was frozen out until Lent, at which point everyone was too cold and too hungry to continue and the matter dissolved over soup. History is unambiguous: nobody has ever resumed a friendship by discussing why it stopped. They resumed it by arriving with soup. Bring soup. Do not bring the subject.1 — T.B.

Dear Professor: I have been offered a promotion that I do not want, and everyone tells me I should be thrilled. What do I do? — Rising, Reluctantly

In 1511 a man in Lower Pemberton was made village reeve against his wishes and served forty years with quiet competence and no joy whatsoever. His successor declined the post, kept his orchard, and is remembered — the records are quite clear — as the happier of the two, though only one of them has a road named after him. History offers you both outcomes and declines to choose. That is what history is for.2 — T.B.

Dear Professor: Is it foolish to be fond of an object? I am sixty-one and I have cried about a bench. — Sentimental, by the Ramp

Consult the Somerset correspondence of 1387, in which a household formally notified its bees of a death, a marriage, and a change in jam supplier. Note the order. Note that the bees took the third hardest. Attachment does not distribute itself according to importance; it distributes itself according to how often a thing has been present. Your bench has been present since 1978. Cry about the bench.3 — T.B.

1. See footnote 3.   2. The orchard is also gone now. This does not affect the argument.   3. See footnote 1.

Departures, Gently

THE 1978 SHIM

Manufactured 1978 — Retired, Unused, 2026

The shim, a small wedge of cedar cut forty-eight years ago for the express purpose of leveling the bench by the boat ramp, has retired without ever having been installed, surrounded by tools that respected it.

Cut in the autumn of 1978 by a carpenter whose name has not survived, the shim was carried to the boat ramp, held up to the gap beneath the bench's left foot, and then — for reasons lost to the record — put back in the apron pocket. It remained in one apron pocket or another for four decades, passed between three carpenters, each of whom understood without being told that it was not to be used.

"It fit," said the carpenter who inherited it last, turning it over on his porch this week, the pie cooling beside him. "That's the thing people won't understand. It fit perfectly. Every one of us checked. And every one of us put it back."

The shim is survived by the wobble, now protected by unanimous vote, and by the bench, which never knew how close it came. It will be kept in the toolbox. "You don't throw out a shim," the carpenter said. "You just don't use it. There's a difference, and it took me thirty years to learn it."
