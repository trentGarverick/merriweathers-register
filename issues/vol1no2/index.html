Front Page · Civic Triumph

TOWN ADMIRES FOOTBRIDGE AT LAST; STRUCTURE REPORTEDLY "DOING ITS BEST"

Council votes 6–0 that the footbridge behind the cannery remains "still charming" — Harold Knapp, who demanded the matter be addressed, admires it twice more for the record

By the News Desk · Behind the Cannery

BEHIND THE CANNERY — For years it stood there, spanning the narrow part of the Mild River where the water slows to consider its options, and for years no one said a word about it. On Thursday, that ended. The Gentle Rapids Town Council voted 6–0, with Councilman Orville Tubb resting productively, to formally declare the footbridge behind the cannery "still charming."

The vote followed a citizen petition, the citizen being Harold Knapp, who has admired the footbridge privately since the Carter administration and felt the time had come to do so out loud. "I wrote to the paper," Mr. Knapp told the council, holding the letter, which had been printed. "I said it was being overlooked. And look at us now."

Council President Verna Dale opened the floor to admiration, and admiration was offered freely. Twelve residents spoke. One praised the footbridge's slats. Another praised the gap between the slats, "which lets you see the water, but only a little, which is the right amount." A third had prepared remarks about the railings and was moved, mid-sentence, to silence.

The footbridge itself could not be reached for comment, on account of being a footbridge. When the question was put to it anyway, by Mr. Knapp, it was reported by those present to be "doing its best," a characterization the council found insufficientdeeply moving and entered into the minutes unanimously.

Parks Director Lyle Greb confirmed the footbridge has required no maintenance in its history. "It just holds," he said, with feeling. "Year after year. You walk across, you get to the other side. That's the whole arrangement, and it has never once broken its word."

Following the vote, the Parks Department deployed confetti, per tradition, some of which settled on the footbridge and was left there. "It looks nice," said Ms. Dale. "It looks like the footbridge is wearing a little something. It earned it."

Mr. Knapp, vindicated, walked to the center of the span, turned to face the cannery, and admired the footbridge twice more — once for the official record and once, he said, "for free." Mayor Birdie Loomis has declared the footbridge a Local Treasure, effective immediately and retroactive to whenever it was built, which no one can quite recall.

MAN FINISHES SECOND BOOK; RETURNS IT ON TIME

Eustace Crabwell completes "Following Through: A Beginner's Guide" before its due date — town delighted, mildly disoriented, unsure of the protocol for punctuality

By the News Desk · The Public Library

THE LIBRARY — Eustace Crabwell, 74, fresh from the parade in his honor last week, walked into the Gentle Rapids Public Library on Monday and returned a book three weeks early. The book, Following Through: A Beginner's Guide, had been due July 8. He had not only returned it on time. He had read it.

"I got to the end," Mr. Crabwell announced, setting it on the counter. "There was a whole second half. I had no idea."

Head librarian Opal Hutchins, who has presided over the desk since 1979, said the early return presented "an unprecedented situation." The library, she explained, has a procedure for books that are late, refined over decades. It has no procedure whatsoever for a book that is early. "We had to look it up," she said. "It turns out you just say thank you. So we did. It felt strange. It felt wonderful."

The marching band, assembled out of habit on the lawn, was uncertain whether a punctual return warranted a parade. After deliberation, band director Felix Mott led them in a single, dignified note, held for as long as the tuba section had breath, "to mark the occasion without overstating it."

Mr. Crabwell, asked what he had learned, grew thoughtful. "The book says the secret to following through is to begin," he said. "And then, crucially, to not stop. I'd been doing the first part for years." He has checked out a third volume, Knowing When You're Done, which he says he intends to return "exactly when it makes sense to."

Ms. Hutchins reports the fine jar remains full of buttons.

—and so I put it to you all directly, and I will not be deterred by the fact that no one has asked.

ITEM THE FIRST: It has come to my attention that the friendliest duck in Gentle Rapids (see last issue, a triumph) bears the name Reginald. It has further come to my attention that the finest man I ever knew, my dear departed mentor, also bore the name Reginald — Reginald Foxworth-Plume, who taught me everything and who, the record now firmly establishes, did not exist.

ITEM THE SECOND: I ask you. I ask the building. Is it not possible — is it not, in fact, beautiful — that the spirit of the man has returned to us in the form of the duck? That Reginald, denied existence in life, has chosen at last to exist as waterfowl?

ITEM THE THIRD: Doris says this is "a lot." Doris says I should "drink some water and sit down." I have done both. I am sitting down with the water now. The question remains.

I have instructed Barty to investigate. He has the tapes. He has, I am told, always had the tapes.

A toast — to Reginald, in all his forms, feathered and otherwise. May he continue to greet the boat ramp.

Yours in inquiry,
C.B.M. III

The Investigative Desk

THE REGINALD QUESTION

One name. One duck. One man who never was. This reporter follows the evidence to the second piling.

By Bartholomew Quill, Esq. · Pulitzer Finalist, 0 Times

THE ASSIGNMENT

The Publisher Emeritus has asked this reporter to determine whether the friendliest duck in Gentle Rapids is, in fact, the reincarnated spirit of Reginald Foxworth-Plume, a man who never existed. This reporter accepted the assignment, as this reporter accepts all assignments: gravely, and with a fresh ribbon in the machine.

THE COMPLICATION

To reincarnate, a thing must first have lived. The records are unanimous: Reginald Foxworth-Plume did not. This reporter spent two decades confirming the nonexistence of lesser men and recognizes the work. Whoever erased Reginald was a professional, or Reginald was never there. The distinction, this reporter notes, may not matter to the duck.

THE INTERVIEW

This reporter approached the duck at the second piling and put the question plainly: "Are you Reginald?" The duck regarded this reporter for a long moment. The duck did not deny it. The duck accepted a piece of bread, which a guilty duck and an innocent duck would both do, rendering the test, this reporter concedes, inconclusive.

THE GOOSE, AGAIN

The goose, reached for follow-up, honked for thirty-one minutes on the subject. This reporter has reviewed the tapes. Within the honking, this reporter believes — believes — there was a moment that sounded like the word "perhaps." This reporter will not overstate it. This reporter has, however, underlined it.

THE FINDING

This reporter finds the following: the duck is named Reginald. The man was named Reginald. One of them greets the boat ramp each morning with uncommon warmth, and one of them taught Mr. Merriweather everything he knows. This reporter is no longer certain these are two different statements. The investigation remains open. The bread budget does not.

The Poetry Corner

Ode to the Footbridge

By Eugene Tippet · Staff Poet

O little bridge behind the plant,
They overlooked you. Now they shan't.
You span the slow and thoughtful part
Where rivers pause to take their heart.

You never asked to be admired,
You simply held, and never tired.
No plaque, no paint, no great ado —
Just slats, and gaps, and getting through.

And should we cross you, you and I,
And reach the other side, and sigh,
We'll know the secret you have kept:
You did your best. And so, you held.

Eugene has been told this is his finest work. — D.P.

THE OLD CANNERY WHISTLE

1924 — 2026

The Gentle Rapids Cannery Whistle, which for one hundred and two years announced noon to a town that already knew, has fallen silent, retiring gently after a long and punctual life.

Installed in 1924 and never once wrong, the whistle blew every day at twelve o'clock sharp, marking the hour for generations of residents who had watches, owned clocks, and could see the sun perfectly well. It was, by every measure, redundant. It was also beloved beyond all reason, which is the only kind of beloved that counts.

"You didn't need it to know it was noon," said one longtime cannery worker. "You needed it to know it was our noon." In its final years the whistle had grown soft, more of a sigh than a blast, and the town leaned in to hear it, which everyone agrees was nicer anyway.

The whistle is survived by the footbridge, its neighbor of a century, who was admired this very week and is said to be taking the loss with quiet dignity. In lieu of flowers, the town will observe noon on its own for a while, and try to make it count. The whistle would have wanted us to look up. Gerald the triceratops, we are told, declined.
